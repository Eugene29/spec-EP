The computing laboratory occupied the upper floor of a building beside the
river. From the windows, the researchers could see delivery trucks crossing
the bridge and small boats moving slowly along the opposite bank. Inside,
the sound was less peaceful. Cooling fans maintained a steady roar behind
the doors of the machine room, where rows of servers worked through jobs
submitted by teams across the university. Some jobs simulated weather,
others analyzed images, and several trained language models on collections
of scientific and technical documents.

On Tuesday morning, Elena arrived with a notebook containing a surprisingly
short question: how much does placement matter? Her team had developed a
distributed experiment that could run on four accelerators. They could place
all four inside one server, or divide them between two servers connected
through the laboratory network. The model, input data, and numerical
precision would remain fixed. They wanted to understand what changed when
some of the model's communication had to leave the local machine.

The question sounded straightforward, but Elena knew that a clean answer
required careful preparation. A distributed program could behave differently
for reasons that had little to do with the network. One server might have
a competing job. Another might load its data from a slower filesystem.
Different library versions could select different kernels. Even an innocent
change to the input passage might alter which experts received the most
work. Before running anything expensive, the team needed a repeatable
procedure and a clear definition of what they intended to measure.

Her colleague Daniel suggested beginning with a small correctness test.
Performance numbers would mean little if the model had not actually been
partitioned as expected. They inspected the process identifiers, checked
which accelerator belonged to each process, and printed the membership of
the communication groups. They also examined the expert layers after
initialization. A configuration file requesting expert parallelism was
useful evidence of intent, but they wanted evidence that the running model
contained the expected distributed layers and local expert parameters.

The first attempt failed before any useful computation began. One process
could not find the input file because it had been launched from a different
working directory. The other processes waited for it to enter a collective
operation. Daniel stopped the job and changed the startup procedure so that
every process checked its inputs before beginning the distributed forward
pass. They also made the error messages include the global rank and file
path. The next failure, if there was one, would be easier to locate.

Once the input was available everywhere, they discussed how to divide it.
Their initial benchmark used one sequence per process, with each sequence
containing exactly five hundred and twelve tokens. The complete experiment
therefore contained four sequences across four processes. They tokenized
one long document and assigned a different consecutive section to each
rank. This avoided padding and gave them distinct inputs without requiring
a separate file for every accelerator. The same sections would be reused
in both placement configurations.

Elena emphasized that characters and tokens were different units. Taking
five hundred characters from a document would not reliably produce five
hundred tokens. Common words, punctuation, technical terms, and whitespace
could all affect tokenization. The team therefore loaded the complete text,
converted it into token identifiers, checked the total token count, and
only then selected the slices. They preserved the leading batch dimension
so that each process supplied a tensor with one row and the chosen number
of token positions.

For the first forward pass, they disabled cached state and did not request
generation. The model would process the supplied sequence once and return
its outputs. This workload resembled the prompt-processing portion of
inference and gave the expert layers a substantial collection of tokens to
route. It did not include backward propagation, optimizer updates, or the
repeated single-token steps used during autoregressive decoding. The team
wrote those boundaries into the experiment notes to prevent later confusion
about what the measurements represented.

The successful smoke test produced an unexpected feeling of relief. There
was no dramatic visualization, only several log messages and tensors with
plausible shapes. Nevertheless, the result established several important
facts at once. All processes had initialized, the input tensors were on the
correct devices, the distributed expert layers had executed, and the job
had completed without a communication mismatch. The researchers saved the
configuration and software versions before moving on to timing.

They began with warmup iterations. The earliest execution could include
memory allocation, lazy initialization, kernel loading, and other setup
costs. Those activities mattered for startup latency, but they were not
the subject of this experiment. The team wanted to characterize repeated
forward passes after the system had reached a stable operating state.
They therefore kept startup measurements separate and made sure every
process completed its warmup before the timed section began.

Timing accelerator work required another small adjustment in perspective.
The host program could submit operations and continue running before the
device finished them. Measuring only the duration of the Python function
call could therefore underestimate the actual work. For their simple
baseline, the researchers synchronized at the measurement boundaries.
They also gathered elapsed times from all participating processes, because
the distributed iteration could not be considered complete while its
slowest participant was still working.

After several repetitions, Daniel plotted the iteration durations. Most
samples clustered closely together, but a few were noticeably slower.
Rather than removing those samples immediately, he checked whether they
corresponded to external activity or recurring events in the program.
A median described typical behavior, while higher percentiles exposed
occasional delays. Both were useful. A system with excellent average
throughput could still be inconvenient if its latency varied widely from
one iteration to the next.

The team then moved the same four-process job across two servers. Each
server contributed two accelerators, and the expert parallel group still
contained four ranks. They deliberately avoided increasing the number of
devices at the same time. If they had compared four local devices with
eight distributed devices, the result would have mixed changes in model
partitioning, total workload, and communication placement. Holding the group
size constant gave them a narrower and more interpretable comparison.

The distributed run completed successfully, but its iteration time was
higher. That observation alone did not explain the cause. Communication
might be slower across the network, yet the extra time could also reflect
different arrival times at collective operations. A rank with more expert
assignments might delay its peers. Small operations might become sensitive
to launch overhead. Some transfers could overlap with computation, while
others might lie directly on the critical path. They needed a timeline
before drawing stronger conclusions.

For profiling, Elena selected a short window of steady-state iterations.
Collecting every event from a long run would produce large traces and
make inspection cumbersome. Each rank wrote its own trace with a unique
filename. The researchers enabled the activities needed to observe host
operations and accelerator kernels, then added more detailed information
only when a specific question justified the overhead. Benchmark timing
and profiling were conducted in separate runs so that instrumentation
would not distort their primary performance numbers.

The first timeline showed alternating regions of dense computation,
routing work, expert computation, and communication. Some operations were
easy to recognize, while others required inspecting their parent calls.
Daniel traced the dispatch of activations toward the owning expert ranks
and the return of expert outputs toward their originating ranks. He was
careful not to add every communication duration together and label the
sum as removable overhead. Operations could overlap, and their contribution
to elapsed time depended on where they appeared in the execution schedule.

A particularly interesting region appeared near the middle of the model.
One rank spent longer in expert computation than its neighbors, and the
others appeared to wait afterward. The network transfer itself was not
necessarily responsible for the full visible delay. The team examined the
number of assignments received by each rank and found an uneven distribution.
This was a useful reminder that expert parallelism involved both moving
data and balancing computation. A faster transport could help without
eliminating every source of waiting.

They also inspected the model's nonexpert components. Attention, shared
experts, normalization, and output projection still consumed time and
memory. An experiment described as an expert parallel benchmark could
be dominated by other operations if its sequence length or implementation
made those components especially expensive. The team kept whole-model
latency as the main outcome, while using layer-level evidence to explain
it. They did not assume that the largest individual kernel was the best
optimization target.

By late afternoon, they were ready to vary the workload. They first
increased sequence length while leaving the per-process batch size fixed.
Longer sequences supplied more token assignments to the expert layers,
but also increased work elsewhere in the model. Next, they considered
increasing batch size at a fixed sequence length. These experiments could
produce the same total number of tokens with different attention shapes,
so the team recorded both dimensions rather than reducing every workload
to a single token count.

The researchers resisted the temptation to change several settings at
once. A different communication backend, a new attention implementation,
and a larger batch might all improve throughput, but a combined change
would make the source of improvement difficult to identify. Their baseline
remained deliberately simple. New configurations would be compared against
it one at a time, with the same inputs, device placement, and measurement
procedure wherever possible. This approach was slower than collecting
many arbitrary numbers, but faster than explaining contradictory results.

At the next meeting, Elena presented the findings as a set of observations
rather than a universal ranking. The local configuration had lower latency
for the workloads they tested. The distributed configuration exposed more
communication and waiting in several expert layers. Larger token batches
changed the balance between computation and transfer. None of these facts
proved that one topology would always be preferable. Larger models, memory
constraints, training workloads, and different networks could change the
practical choice.

Their eventual goal was training, which introduced additional questions.
Backward propagation would generate gradients and reverse some activation
flows. Replicated parameters would require appropriate synchronization.
Activation checkpointing could repeat parts of the forward computation,
and optimizer state would alter the memory budget. The inference experiment
had established a useful foundation, but it had not measured those costs.
The next phase would preserve the verified setup while adding training
components in controlled steps.

Before leaving, Daniel organized the experiment directory. It contained
the launch commands, configuration files, input passage, software versions,
timing summaries, and selected profiler traces. He added a short explanation
of the rank layout on each server and the exact definition of reported
throughput. Someone returning a month later should be able to reproduce
the experiment without reconstructing its assumptions from memory. Good
records were part of the result, not merely administrative work afterward.

Outside, the evening light reflected from the river onto the laboratory
windows. The team had answered a smaller question than the one they began
with, but they had answered it carefully. They knew that the model executed
with the intended expert partitioning, that both placements used matched
inputs, and that their measurements represented completed distributed
forward passes. More importantly, they now had a method for investigating
the next question without losing track of what each experiment could
actually establish.
